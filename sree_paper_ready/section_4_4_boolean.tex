\label{sec:boolean}
For boolean-control variables we probe the syntactic \emph{occurrence type}
of each flag site---assignment, conditional use, loop use, or return
use---from mean-pooled residual activations at the flag's own tokens, on
XLCoST Python under the protocol of Section~3: problem-grouped frozen
splits, validation-selected layers, five seeds, and every probe reported
against model-free baselines on the identical occurrence sample.

\paragraph{Occurrence type is decodable at ceiling---and so is the source
line.} Across three models spanning two families and a $4.5\times$ scale
range, probes reach macro-F1 $0.977$--$0.989$ against a majority baseline
of $0.174$ (Table~\ref{tab:boolean-baselines}). Selectivity over a
Hewitt-style control task \citep{hewitt2019control} is positive and stable
($+0.189$ to $+0.203$), so the probe is reading representation rather than
memorising at this sample size. However, a character $n$-gram classifier
over the \emph{enclosing source line} with the variable masked---no model
involved---reaches macro-F1 $0.988$ on the same occurrences and fold. All
three probes sit within one test occurrence of that line ($-0.011$,
$-0.003$, $+0.0005$, against a resolution of $\rho = 0.0154$;
Table~\ref{tab:resolution}), so we report them as indistinguishable from
it rather than ranking them. Macro-F1 does rise across the three models---
$0.977$ to $0.985$ with code pretraining at fixed scale, and $0.989$ at
$4.5\times$ the parameters---but each increment is smaller than $\rho$,
and none of the three carries the probe past what the masked line already
achieves without a model. Occurrence type is
carried by local syntax that is fully present in the surface form; linear
decodability here is not evidence of computation beyond it. A static
analyser decides this label exactly, so the question of interest is not
whether the information is present but \emph{what carries it}---which the
renaming counterfactuals isolate.

\paragraph{Renaming does not move the probe.} We re-extract activations
for five naming counterfactuals applied by scope-correct span edits with
occurrence identities preserved (Section~3). Each condition is drawn from
the same frozen occurrence sample as the baseline, less the occurrences
its rewrite legitimately cannot produce: the rewriter refuses functions
whose scopes it cannot handle exactly and drops programs failing the
re-parse or identity gates, retaining $98.5\%$ of the sample for C1/C2/C4
and $97.7\%$ for C3/C5. Attrition is structural---driven by program scope
constructs, not by the label or the probe's behaviour---and paired deltas
are computed on the resulting shared intersection, with the comparison
refused outright below $90\%$ overlap.

Table~\ref{tab:boolean-renaming} reports those deltas. Every one is within
$\pm 0.016$ macro-F1, across all five conditions and all three models---
including C5, which renames each flag to an index-style identifier
(\texttt{i}, \texttt{idx}, \texttt{pos}), the same manipulation that costs
the index role $\Delta\mathrm{F1} = -0.272$ (Section~\ref{sec:index}). The
largest delta in the table, $-0.016$, is one test occurrence at Python's
resolution ($\rho = 0.0154$); the median, $-0.004$, is a quarter of one.
Every renaming effect this manipulation produces therefore lies at or
below the smallest difference the design can measure, while the index
effect is roughly $18$ occurrences and far above it. Boolean occurrence
type is \textbf{name-invariant} in the only sense the evidence supports:
five manipulations that remove, collapse, or adversarially invert lexical
information move the probe by less than the measurement's own step size.
We make no claim about the signs or the ordering of the individual deltas,
and none about the effect being exactly zero---a real effect smaller than
$\rho$ would be invisible here. Distinguishing that case from true
invariance needs an uncapped, class-stratified sample, which we did not
run.

\paragraph{Placement on the dissociation axis.} The three roles now occupy
distinct, replicated positions under one corpus and one manipulation:
index collapses under misleading names ($\Delta\mathrm{F1}=-0.272$),
accumulator improves ($+0.079$), and boolean is invariant
($|\Delta\mathrm{F1}| \le 0.016$ across three models). Combined with the
baseline analysis above, the boolean case also sharpens the interpretation
of the axis itself: name-invariance alone does not imply an abstract role
representation---here the invariant signal is local syntax, available in
the enclosing line and decodable from the earliest layers. Cross-role
comparisons of probe F1 should therefore be read jointly with surface
baselines, not against majority chance alone.

\paragraph{Replication in three further languages.} Java, JavaScript, and
PHP reproduce the profile under the same protocol
(Table~\ref{tab:boolean-crosslang}). In each, the masked \emph{statement}
baseline is the strongest model-free predictor---XLCoST's detokenized
programs in brace languages are single-line, so the enclosing statement
rather than the line is the local-surface unit---and in each the
information is complete by the early layers. None of the three supports
the renaming counterfactuals, which are Python-only (Section~3), so they
extend the surface-decodability result and not the name-invariance one.

\paragraph{How large a difference can this design resolve?}
Macro-F1 on a four-class problem is hostage to its smallest class, and the
PROTOCOL occurrence cap of $2{,}000$---sampled as whole problems, which
preserves the class skew---leaves those classes very small. We therefore
report, for each language, the resolution $\rho$: the macro-F1 movement
caused by a \emph{single} test occurrence of the smallest class changing
its prediction (Table~\ref{tab:resolution}, measured directly on the real
test folds rather than approximated). The consequence is stark. In Python
and JavaScript, \emph{every} probe-minus-baseline margin in
Table~\ref{tab:boolean-crosslang} is smaller than one test instance:
Python's three margins are $-0.7$, $-0.2$, and $+0.0$ instances,
JavaScript's are $-0.1$, $+0.3$, and $-0.2$. Those margins carry no
information about which predictor is better, and we do not interpret their
signs. What survives is the comparison the design \emph{can} make: probes
and the masked-statement baseline are indistinguishable at this
resolution, in every language tested.

PHP is the one exception, and for an instructive reason. Its extraction
pool ($1{,}774$ occurrences) falls below the cap, so no capping is
applied, and its smallest test class retains $22$ occurrences against
Python's $9$ and JavaScript's $6$. It is therefore the \emph{finest}
resolution in the study despite the smallest corpus, and it is the only
language where all three probes exceed the surface baseline by more than
one instance ($+2.2$, $+0.9$, $+3.4$), with the highest selectivity
observed ($+0.214$ to $+0.240$). We flag this as the single result
warranting follow-up rather than claiming it: three instances out of
twenty-two is a fragile margin, and it needs the per-condition intervals
and an uncapped replication in another language before it can support any
claim that PHP representations carry occurrence-type information beyond
the local surface. The broader lesson is that the cap, not the corpus, is
the binding constraint---Python's smallest test class falls from $87$
occurrences to $9$ under it, coarsening $\rho$ roughly tenfold.

\paragraph{Corpus quality.} Two defects in XLCoST bear on these numbers
and we state them rather than let them sit in the artifacts. First,
$14.2\%$ of JavaScript programs ($1{,}179$ of $8{,}564$) fail to parse
after detokenization, against $0.9\%$ for Python and $0.1\%$ for Java.
Inserting a single \texttt{||} at the error position repairs $318$ of
them; missing statement terminators account for most of the rest, since
JavaScript's automatic semicolon insertion requires newlines that the
single-line detokenization removes. Failing programs are longer than
surviving ones (median $484$ versus $375$ characters), so JavaScript's
retained sample is biased toward shorter programs. Second, the
detokenization leaves SentencePiece word markers (U+2581) in a third of
the Java, PHP, and C\# programs and in $70\%$ of C---only Python, C++, and
JavaScript are clean. We tested whether this depresses the PHP baseline
and it does not: the masked-statement baseline scores $0.953$ on
contaminated PHP programs versus $0.944$ on clean ones, so PHP's lower
baseline is a property of the language, not of the artifact.
